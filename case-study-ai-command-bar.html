AI Command Bar

One week to build it. Two months to find out what it should be.

Hudl

During Hudl's annual one-week skunkworks event, my coworker Gabby wanted to build an AI-powered search bar just to learn how to build with AI. I already suspected we'd need real filter-search for our upcoming Volleyball Consolidation work, so we built toward that instead of a demo. A week later we convinced our PM to ship the V1 straight to production. I've spent the eight months since watching real search queries come in to learn what people actually want from it.

The Vision

It’s AI, but subtle. It doesn’t talk back. It just does.

Users can find exactly what they’re looking for fast, accurately, and using their own words.

It’s not just a search bar to look for clips, but a new way to command the UI.

Designing V1 in five days

Skunkworks weeks don’t come with research, a spec, or a second designer. What they come with is seven engineers who signed up to learn how to build with AI, making a dozen small calls an hour while I’m in one conversation at a time. So the first thing I made wasn’t a screen. It was the vision above and the four principles below it — what the team could settle arguments against when I wasn’t in the room.

Speak the user’s language

Match how people actually phrase things, not how our data is structured.

Encourage exploration

Make it safe to poke around instead of requiring the exact right query.

Make it accurate

Results have to be trustworthy, or people stop bothering to search.

Make it fast

Speed is part of the experience, not a performance afterthought.

Then the design itself, decided in hours rather than weeks: where it lives, what it looks like, and the states it moves through.

Placement

It went into the top bar, in line with the filter count and the match name — not a docked panel, not an overlay. Anything sitting beside the video takes room from the video, and the video is the only reason a coach opened the page. In the chrome, search is always one keystroke away and never in the way.

Color

The field is the only thing in that entire bar carrying color; everything around it is grayscale. A gradient sweeps its border while it thinks. If the AI isn’t going to say anything, color is how it tells you it’s there and that it’s working.

Empty and typing states

An unlabeled field teaches nobody anything, and we had no room for a tutorial. The placeholder was the entire instruction set — enough to suggest the field would take a sentence, without promising more than we could deliver in a week.

Type-ahead

The moment you type, the field offers readings of what you’ve written, each with the number of moments behind it: your attacks, 29. To zone 4, 11. In first ball, 13. That was the answer to encourage exploration — you learn what the system can do by watching it guess, rather than by reading help text.

By Friday we had something that worked end to end.

Getting it onto the roadmap

Skunkworks projects are supposed to die on Friday. This one was never scoped, never staffed and never meant to ship — so I spent the back half of the week arguing that it should anyway.

Design-led, not spec-led

Nobody handed us a brief. Gabby wanted to learn how to build with AI, and I pointed that at a gap I already knew Volleyball Consolidation was going to hit. The direction came out of design, and seven engineers built toward it.

Designed enough to be safe

Not finished — finished was never the bar. Placement, states and type-ahead were resolved, the failure paths were understood, and it had been tested. Enough that putting it in front of real coaches wasn’t reckless.

Shipped as a question

We pushed it live to see what would happen. A demo can only ever tell you whether something looks good. Production was the only way to find out whether anyone wanted it, and what they’d want from it next.

The roadmap change

A week that was meant to end in a demo ended in a shipped feature and a roadmap item nobody had planned — and the search foundation the consolidation work needed a quarter later.

Two months of reading real searches

Then I ran the research we couldn’t run up front, because now there was something to run it on. 2,239 searches from 1,168 coaches, athletes and staff — every one of them typed by someone who wanted something, with no researcher in the room.

How I did it

A script, not a spreadsheet

2,239 queries is too many to sort by hand and too few to hand to a model and trust. I used AI to write and refine a Python script that tagged every query against our own data schema.

AI to build the tool, not to do the job

The output is rules I can read, not a judgment I made 2,239 times. When the categories changed, I re-ran it in seconds instead of re-reading everything.

Coded twice

Once for what the person was trying to do, once for whether we handed back the right thing. The second pass is the one that found the problem.

Numbers I could argue from

Deterministic tagging meant I could take the findings into a roadmap conversation without anyone asking whether I’d just picked the examples that suited me.

What I found

They were using it as a command bar

Only 63.5% was filtering. The rest asked the product to do things — start a job, open a report, change the video. One in nine weren’t in the right match at all; someone typed “harriton basketball” while sitting inside a volleyball game.

It was right, but not reliably

87% of searches came back with the correct filters. The other 13% failed quietly — and accuracy collapsed on anything we couldn’t filter on, down to 33% for reports and 0% for highlights.

No view of the result

You typed a sentence and the video list changed. Nothing told you what had been narrowed or how much was left, so there was no way to judge whether you’d got what you asked for.

No view of the thinking

And nothing showed which filters it had chosen. A wrong guess and an empty match looked identical, which meant a bad result was indistinguishable from a bad question.

The last two are the same problem, and they’re the reason the 13% hurt more than it should have. Being wrong 13% of the time is survivable. Being wrong 13% of the time invisibly is what makes people stop trusting a feature. So I stopped designing a filter box and started designing a command surface, and took three changes to the team.

Three changes I argued for

1. Data chips

The gap

Type-ahead told you how many. It never told you what. Nothing showed the filters behind a suggestion until you’d already committed to it.

The fix

Every reading now carries its filters — #4 H Monk Attacks to Zone 4 — eleven moments. A wrong guess is visible before it costs you your place, and the chips stay after you commit.

2. Less AI

“Hannah’s attacks” doesn’t need a model

A player plus an event, resolvable against a data schema we already own. The model gets player names right 91.7% of the time; a fuzzy match against the roster can only ever return names that exist.

Match first, model second

The AI gets saved for what only it can do — judgment, relationships across a rally, math asked out loud. Everything else resolves instantly, for nothing, while you’re still typing.

What it costs to over-use a model

$1.03 per 1,000 searches and a 1.07s average wait — cheap today, but spent on the two thirds that never needed it, and both numbers scale with volume.

The same move I made on my own research

Use the model to build the deterministic thing, then let the deterministic thing do the work. Only 2.1% of searches genuinely need one.

3. New destinations

People were already using it as a command bar. The design hadn’t caught up.

Start a workflow

Identify unknowns. Tag the game. Split tool.

Open a report

Spray chart. Attack tendencies.

Change the content

Past seasons, another team, the whole library.

Not a model problem

“Big screen” doesn’t need AI. It needs somewhere to go.

Data chips

Shipped

Ranked readings, visible chips, counts before you commit.

New destinations

In progress

Workflows, reports and content changes routed out of the field.

Less AI

Proposed

Schema match first, model second. Argument made, work not yet done.

What I’d do differently

Instrument on day one

We had logs from launch, but I built the coding scheme two months in — so the earliest queries got sorted by someone who already had a hunch about what she’d find.

Name it later

We called it AI Filters, an honest description of a one-week build. It quietly became a ceiling on what people thought to ask it for.
