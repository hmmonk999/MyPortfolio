Volleyball Consolidation Part 1 of 3

Reports

Three products, three sets of loyal coaches, one system to replace all of them

Hudl

Hudl, Volleymetrics, and BallTime each shipped their own volleyball reports, and coaches were fiercely loyal to whichever one they learned first. Over a year and 30–40 coach interviews, I designed the system that replaces all three — one layout system, three new components, built around coaches’ habits instead of against them.

The Big Question

How do you replace three reports coaches already love that are all a little different from each other with one system they’ll love more?

Coaches don’t start with video. They start with a report.

I talked to 30–40 coaches over a year, middle school through Olympic analysts. Reports kept sliding on and off the roadmap, so I used the delay to talk to as many coaches as I could — and it was easy to find users since coaches will always make time to talk about their favorite report.

Every one of them described the same loop that they did not like: Click a cell → find the clip → go back → lose your place → repeat.

The problem I didn’t want to repeat

Click a cell → find the clip → go back → lose your place → repeat.

They loved the data. They loved that the data got them to the video, but they didn’t like that it took them out of what they were experiencing — a report-first world.

Familiarity beats almost everything else

Coaches are loyal to what they know, and every change costs them something. But their loyalty wasn’t a reason to repeat the old reports’ mistakes. My PM and I turned the research into a ranked priority guide, so the team could settle tradeoffs without me in the room.

1. Path to action

Coaches don’t just read reports. They clean data and find video in them.

2. Familiarity

Years of muscle memory. Don’t spend it.

3. Interactive-ness

Data is better when the video is one click away.

4. Responsiveness

Athletes live on their phones. Hudl reports didn’t.

Where coaches look for reports

Before designing anything, I mapped where each product hid its reports — which nav slot, and what it was called. All three buried it in nearly the same place. So we left it there.

This was the key to keeping reports familiar, while adding new features and making some changes to layouts and components. How do you replace three reports coaches already love with one they’ll love even more? You keep their wayfinding the same—a lesson, unfortunately, we had to learn the hard way (more context below).

Three components, a header, and a layout system covered every report we had

Then I mapped every existing report and its flow, plus what competitors shipped, so anything we cut could come back as a planned fast follow instead of a surprise.

Everything we owed coaches collapsed into three components: a data table worth staring at, a location chart with lines and heat maps, and a trends chart that redraws on demand. A full report builder needed more than that — those became follow-ups.

We built the system. We built the components. 2 mistakes almost cost us everything.

One we were forced into. One was an existing system that I advocated to change.

We shipped reports before they were ready. A new data system forced us into a video-first approach. User sentiment was rough...

Familiarity

We never meant to ship video-first. The data behind the old reports broke in a way we couldn’t repair. Another initiative needed that data pipeline that broke the old reports — so video-first went out early: pick your content, then see your numbers.

It turned into the clearest evidence we got all year. Coaches didn’t adapt to it. They worked around it, and they told us so. What they wanted was the order the research had described from the start: open reports, see the most recent match, change the content from there.

Rerouting the Reports button to the new experience was always the plan. Shipping the wrong order first just told us exactly what that order was worth.

The existing layout system made reports too easy to dissolve

Path to action Familiarity

I pushed very hard to get this fixed. Our principal designer’s grand vision was a video and data experience that was modular and fluid. The system wasn’t working, and it was one of the core complaints from users. Based on my research, I knew how important it was to get rid of the report-to-video loop and instead have them in one experience. Our existing system did that, but it was only grounded in 1 of the 2 report -> data loop problem (the removal of the loop). It did not help users stay in the reports experience. It still forced them into a video first experience.

The fluidness of the system was forcing users to flip-flop between reports-first and video-first. The fluidness was too jarring.

So I argued to constrain the system. Sometimes a full-screen report should just be a full-screen report — no collapse, no side panel, no transition, even though the system made it possible. Full screen reports should be full screen reports with a hint of video. Full screen video should have a some reports.

Everything below is built on what those two cost us: let coaches start where they already start, and don’t move them once they’re there.

The system we built

Reports Header

One header, every report

Familiarity

Team, matches, and the tabs between report types — in the same place all three old products put them. A coach opening the new system doesn’t hunt for anything. The frame was already familiar, even when the data underneath was new.

Reports Layouts System

A layout system, not one-off reports

Familiarity Responsiveness

We couldn’t rebuild every report for every sport, and other squads were about to start building their own.

So we defined the four layouts volleyball needed and made it easy to add more. Designers and engineers plug components into cards; the system handles responsiveness and spacing. Nobody reinvents a report again — with guardrails, added later, on what a layout isn’t allowed to do.

New Components

The data table

Familiarity Path to action

The same stats in the same shape coaches already read — but every cell is a door into the video behind it. Selections hold, so you can see what you’ve clicked and act on a set of cells instead of chasing one clip and losing your place on the way back.

The location chart

Interactive-ness

Where the ball actually went: serve and attack lines, heat maps for the patterns a column of numbers can’t show. Click a hot zone, get the rallies that made it — which is the only reason a coach is looking at it.

The trends chart

Interactive-ness Familiarity

Is this getting better or worse? The question every coach asks after a match, answered across a season instead of a single game. Pick the stat, the grouping, the window — the same component covers a team’s side-out all year and one athlete’s last five sets.

The Final Product

Rather than screenshot the report, I rebuilt it here. The navigation is the coach’s starting point: which match, the filters over it, and the three things they do with a report once they have it — ask for help, send it to an assistant, take it away as a file.
